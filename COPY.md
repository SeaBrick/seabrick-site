## Building blocks for a rising sea.

      
SeaBrick is a low-cost, carbon-negative interlocking brick system for marine and flood-resistant infrastructure. It is also a nonprofit research program to create sustainable floating infrastructure that coastal communities can build themselves: docks, platforms, breakwaters and the foundations of buildings that rise with the water instead of fighting it.

### One small shape. Any size of structure.

      
A SeaBrick is a hollow hexagon that floats. Bricks lock side to side into a mat that is strong but flexible, like chainmail. Courses stack, like Lego with hooked sides. Lay them flat for a platform. Stack them in rings for a tube or a tower. Tie the courses together with cables and the whole thing moves as one body in the water.

      The material
      

### A brick that turns to stone.

      
The material is a light mineral foam inside a dense mineral shell, with organic fibre throughout. The foam floats. The shell carries the load where bricks grip one another. The fibres mineralize over time into an internal scaffolding that gives the brick strength and flexibility, and the brick gets stronger as it ages.

# Page: mission

Mission
  

## Low-cost, climate-resilient infrastructure for a rising sea.

  
SeaBrick Society's mission is to build floating, water-resistant infrastructure that coastal communities facing rising sea levels can build and own themselves.

  
    

### The water is coming, and concrete is the wrong answer

    
One third of the world's people live in coastal communities. Rising sea levels will destroy the homes of hundreds of millions of them over the next thirty years. Heat, fire and storms will displace billions more. We need low-cost ways of producing resilient infrastructure that does not rely on concrete.
    
There are no renewable products in the marine infrastructure market. Everything is concrete, steel or petroleum. Cement production alone accounts for 9% of global CO2 emissions. The way we build in the sea is part of the problem it is meant to solve.

    

### Build into the ocean instead of retreating from it

    
A floating structure rises with the water. It does not need deep foundations, a dry dock or heavy equipment. SeaBrick lets cities and coastal communities build out into the water, replacing land rather than losing it. Docks, platforms and breakwaters come first. Floating homes and whole communities come after. Bricks are just the means to that end.

    

### Carbon negative, by design

    
Organic fibre is cellulose, and cellulose is about 40% carbon. Each SeaBrick stores more than its own weight in CO2, locked into a mineral matrix for the life of the structure. The opposite of floating concrete and steel.

    

### A hard line on sustainability

    
The question we keep asking is how inexpensive and how scalable we can get while holding a hard line on sustainability. No petrochemicals as an input. No coatings that can shed microparticles into the sea. Materials that mineralize and strengthen over time instead of corroding. That line has cost us time. We keep it.

    

### Community ownership, community control

    
The long-term vision is a decentralized, cooperative manufacturing ecosystem. Low-tech production plants that ship in a container to wherever they are needed, so that communities affected by climate change can build their own floating infrastructure from materials at hand. The tools for adaptation belong in the hands of the people most affected. Long-term ownership held by the communities themselves, and in Canada by Indigenous communities in particular.

    

### What the Society does

    
      

#### Research

Materials, connections and marine durability, with university partners and independent engineers. We publish what we learn, including what did not work.
      

#### The standard

What a SeaBrick is: its dimensions, its connections and its tests. The Society holds the form so that anyone can build with it.
      

#### Pilots

Small real structures with coastal partners: work floats, aquaculture platforms, docks. Working infrastructure first.
      

#### Community

Open days, model bricks, students and advisors. A building system people can hold in their hands is one they can understand.
    
    
Where to from here? The technology shows how the bricks fit together. Get in touch if you want to build with them.

## A brick, and a system for building with it.

  
On its own a SeaBrick is a small thing. Locked to thousands of others it is a platform, a breakwater, a dock or a foundation. This page has two parts: the shape and how bricks fit together, and the material. How both got here is on the evolution page.

### Geometry and design

    
      
The SeaBrick, final design, 2026. The 3D models shown are simplified versions of the brick.
      
        The unit
        

#### Hexagonal, hollow, buoyant.

        
A SeaBrick is a hexagon about one and a half times as wide as it is tall. Hexagons tile with no gaps and give every brick six equal neighbours. Each side locks smoothly to the next brick. The top and bottom register with the course above and below. The hollow inside makes it float; the dense shell carries the loads where bricks meet.
        
A brick is moulded in one cycle, so it can be made in a small plant close to where it is used. The design has been through more than twenty versions since 2022. The evolution page shows them.

#### Vertical tension cables

Bricks resist compression well and tension poorly. Vertical cables run through the holes at the corners of the bricks, from the top course to the bottom, and squeeze the courses tightly together. That turns a stack of loose layers into a rigid structure that carries point loads and holds together in a swell. Cables are tightened, inspected and replaced from above.

#### Gel-sealed cavities

Where two bricks lock together there is a gap. Fill that gap with a gel and let it harden, and the joint is sealed: the mat becomes watertight and the cavity between the bricks gives more buoyancy. Tension cables make the structure rigid; the gel makes it impermeable.

### Material technology

    Materials

### A light mineral core, a dense mineral shell, organic fibre throughout.

    
The material has two jobs that pull against each other. It has to float, and it has to carry the forces that pass between bricks. We give each job to a different part of the brick.
    
      

#### Core

A low-density mineral foam. It supplies most of the buoyancy. The lighter the core, the more a structure can carry.
      

#### Shell

A denser mineral layer that protects the core and carries contact loads, thickest where bricks grip one another. A mineral coating goes over it.
      

#### Fibre

Organic fibre remains an important part of the composite. The fibres mineralize into an internal scaffolding which gives the brick strength and flexibility. Flax, hemp and kelp all work.
    
    
Kelp fibre composites (2021 to 2023), flax and hemp (2025), and mineral foam with fibre (2026). Read more, or see how the material evolved.
    

#### Why mineral

    
Mineral binders cure without the clinker that gives Portland cement its carbon footprint, and seawater does not eat them the way it eats reinforced concrete. Mineralizing agents mixed with the fibres crystallize as water slowly permeates, so the brick becomes a self-healing, stone-like matrix and gets stronger with age. The fibre inside is sequestered carbon: each brick stores more than its own weight in CO2.

When the UBC results showed the kelp composite was about as dense as water, the brick had to become hollow to float well. In the second half of July the lock face changed character: the flat top became a field of small facets around a central boss, so that courses register on each other at many points instead of a few.     Part 2

### Material technology, 2021 to 2026

    
      
        

#### 2021 to 2023: kelp

        
SeaBrick began as an idea about kelp: a feedstock that grows two feet a day and stores carbon, harvested and pressed into a building block. Six UBC Materials Engineering students took it on as a Capstone project in 2022 and made a kelp fibre composite stronger than concrete in compression. A research agreement with UBC's BioProducts Institute followed. The composite was named Kelpstone, and small specimens reached three times the compressive strength of concrete at half the weight. Its density, close to water, is why the brick became hollow.
        

#### 2024 to 2025: other fibres, and keeping water out

        
Work in Western Australia showed that sargassum worked as well as kelp, with a simpler process. When seaweed fibre could not be bought at the scale a pilot needed, flax and hemp from the Prairies were tested in 2025 and maxed out the compression press. In parallel, an Ocean Supercluster grant funded a study of a mineral waterproofing method that needs no petrochemicals and sheds no microparticles.
        

#### 2026: mineral foam and fibre

        
The current material turns the composite inside out. A low-density mineral foam core gives buoyancy. A dense mineral shell carries the loads where bricks meet. Organic fibre runs through both and mineralizes over time into an internal scaffolding that gives strength and flexibility. The brick turns to stone, and gets stronger as it does. ## SeaBrick Society

  
A Canadian nonprofit with one mission: low-cost, climate-resilient infrastructure for the coastal communities that need it most.

  

### What we are

  
SeaBrick Society is a federally incorporated Canadian not-for-profit, based in Vancouver. It holds the SeaBrick brick and coordinates the research, the partnerships and the testing that will take it from a model on a table to infrastructure on the water.
  

### What we are for

  
Floating, water-resistant infrastructure that coastal communities facing rising seas can build and own themselves. Docks, platforms and breakwaters first. Then the foundations of homes and communities that rise with the water instead of fighting it. Carbon negative, made from materials at hand, in plants small enough to ship in a container. The mission says why.
  

### Work with us

  
Researchers and students: materials, marine engineering, naval architecture, manufacturing. Coastal communities and partners: if you need working infrastructure on the water and would consider a pilot, we would like to talk. Builders and makers: the model bricks exist so that people can try the system. Get in touch.
  
  
SeaBrick Society was founded by John Richardson in 2022. It has attracted the interest and support of dozens of engineers, designers, researchers and climate activists, along with funding support from government and charitable organizations including the Government of British Columbia, Canada's Ocean Supercluster, Third Derivative and the Ocean Impact Organization.

## A paper trail: SeaBrick's patent history

  
What has been filed, and when.

A nonprofit that wants to set a standard has an awkward relationship with patents. An open standard works best when anyone can use it. An idea with no protection gets fenced off by whoever moves fastest. SeaBrick's answer has been to file, to keep the filings in the project's hands, and to be plain about what they cover.

### The first filing covered the block, the material and the joint

Patent counsel was engaged in January 2022. On 10 March 2022 a United States provisional application was filed under the title "Kelp-Based Modular Construction Blocks and Methods of Making Same", with SeaBrick Society as applicant. It covered the blocks, the compressed-biomass material and the way the blocks join. The founder called the draft "sixty pages of awesomeness" at the time.

A year later, on 10 March 2023, the international application was filed through the Canadian Intellectual Property Office, and it was published on 14 September 2023 as WO2023168537A1. National applications followed in Canada and the United States in September 2024.

### The 2024 filings belong to the kelp years

Three provisional applications were filed in early 2024: on kelp farm structures, on methods of making seaweed composites (the Kelpstone process developed with UBC), and on a container-sized apparatus for processing biomass in remote communities. They come from the period when the project expected to build the kelp supply chain itself.

### The 2025 filing describes the brick as it is now

On 9 December 2025 a new provisional application, "Modular Construction Blocks and Methods of Making Same", was filed on the component brick and the current approach to moulding. At more than two hundred pages it is the fullest description of the system so far. Trademark applications for the SeaBrick name exist in Canada and the United States.

If you are thinking of building on the SeaBrick system, write to us. Making that possible is the point of the Society.

# Page: journal/keeping-the-water-out

2025 · Research
  

## Keeping the water out

  
A floating brick lives or dies by what water does to it. The 2025 waterproofing study, a brick that turns to stone, and gel between the bricks are three answers to the same question.

Every buoyant material has the same enemy. Water finds the pores, the seams and the places where two parts meet. Over months it adds weight. Over years it takes strength. For a plant-fibre composite the problem is sharper still, because the fibres themselves swell.

### We set a bar that most marine coatings fail

From the start the rule was that whatever keeps water out must not rely on petrochemicals and must not shed microparticles into the sea. Plenty of marine coatings work. Few meet that bar. We hope to set a high bar for environmental safety, and we have kept it.

### The 2025 study gave us our first water-uptake data

With the Ocean Supercluster grant, a team of UBC Materials Engineering students began in January 2025 under two of the project's advisors, Colton Routtenberg and Zachary Huser. The brief was to validate a mineral-based waterproofing: a treatment that bonds to the composite and closes its surface without an organic film. The study produced treated and untreated specimens for immersion testing and gave the project its first systematic data on water uptake.

In the same year the manufacturing side solved a related problem: a simple two-part mould that presses fibre into an eight-inch hexagonal brick. It was heavy, at 80% the density of water, but it floated just fine. The current brick proportions come from there.

### A brick that turns to stone

The better answer came from inside the material. Soak the fibre in a silicate solution and the mineralizing agents crystallize as water slowly permeates, forming a self-healing, stone-like matrix. The brick petrifies over a decade or so, and gets stronger as it does. Waterproofing becomes something the brick does to itself, not a coating that wears off.

### Gel between the bricks seals the structure

The last answer works at the scale of the whole structure. Where two bricks lock together there is a gap. Fill the gap with a gel and let it harden, and the joint is sealed: the mat becomes watertight, and the sealed cavities between the bricks add buoyancy. Tension cables make a structure rigid; the gel makes it impermeable. The cutaway on the technology page shows it.

# Page: journal/mineral-foam-and-fibre

2026 · Materials
  

## Mineral foam and fibre: where the material stands

  
The SeaBrick material is a light mineral foam inside a dense mineral shell, with organic fibre throughout that mineralizes into an internal scaffold. Here is the thinking.

This article paraphrases the materials strategy of October 2026. It gives the thinking; the numbers will come as the tests do.

### The material has two jobs that pull against each other

A floating brick must be light enough to give useful buoyancy and strong enough to carry the forces that pass between bricks when a platform flexes in a swell. Fibre composites did the second job well. They were never going to do the first on their own, and they brought the problem of water with them. The strategy that emerged through 2026 gives each job to a different part of the brick.

### A light core and a dense shell, with fibre throughout

The core is a mineral foam: a low-density mineral composite that supplies most of the buoyancy. The lighter the core, the more the structure can carry.

The shell is a denser mineral layer around the core, thickest where bricks grip one another. It protects the core, gives a hard surface at the joints and carries contact loads. The outer surface of a whole structure is coated independently of the bricks, the way a ship's hull is, with a cladding that can be replaced every few years.

The fibre remains an important part of the composite. The fibres mineralize into an internal scaffolding which gives the brick strength and flexibility. Flax, hemp and kelp all work; the choice is about what grows nearby.

### Why mineral

Mineral binders cure without the clinker that gives Portland cement its carbon footprint, and seawater does not eat them the way it eats reinforced concrete. Mineralizing agents mixed with the fibre crystallize as water slowly permeates, so the brick becomes a self-healing, stone-like matrix and gets stronger with age. The fibre inside is sequestered carbon: each brick stores more than its own weight in CO2. The question we keep asking is how inexpensive and how scalable we can get while holding a hard line on sustainability.

### What the next round tests

The interlocking brick shape is finalized. The next round of work tests the combination of foam core, protective shell, waterproofing, curing and small-batch production at full size, in marine conditions;

Density, dry and conditioned, and how much it varies from batch to batch.

Water uptake under immersion, pressure and wet-dry cycling.

Shrinkage, fibre toughness and the bond between core and shell through curing and ageing.

Placing and demoulding one finished brick per cycle from a two-part mould.

The kelp composite and the flax and hemp work are what taught us about fibre, binders, moisture and supply. The mineral foam is where that learning has arrived.

# Page: journal/the-shape-of-a-brick

2022 to 2026 · Design
  

## The shape of a brick

  
Five years, more than twenty versions and a few thousand 3D prints: how the SeaBrick went from a square slab to a hexagonal system brick.

A SeaBrick has to do three things at once. It has to float. It has to lock to its neighbours well enough that a platform of thousands behaves like one body. And it has to come out of a mould cheaply, anywhere in the world. Most of the design history is those three requirements arguing with each other. The pictures are rendered from the original design files; the evolution page has the full set, the walkthrough videos and the photographs.

### 2022 began with a square and ended with a hexagon

The first 3D designs were drawn by industrial designer Rico Jay Beringuela in January 2022 and printed within days at 3DSMITH, a small Vancouver studio, at ten centimetres across. The drawings for the first patent, in March 2022, already showed a family: square, triangle, hexagon, rhombus, trapezoid and a "mini". Unlike Lego, which is built on a square, SeaBrick uses a triangular pattern, so the family tiles together. Through the year the versions ran past twenty as the lock and the wall thickness were refined. The first hexagon went to the printer in November. Every design since has been a hexagon.

### The material made the brick hollow

When the UBC results showed the composite itself was about as dense as water, the brick became a two-part shell with hollow cells inside. A February 2023 walkthrough video documented the lock of that generation, a set of bumps and tongues, and plaster prototypes were cast that spring to try it by hand. The brick is moulded in two halves, top and bottom, which is how the internal honeycomb is made.

One-edge square, v24

### The hinge-lock replaced force with a turn

In October 2023 the bump-and-tongue lock gave way to the hinge-lock, developed over the following year with 3D printing advisor Brady Kirk and designer Xiaoyuan Geng. Bricks could now be joined and separated with a rotating motion instead of brute force. In September 2024 the hinge-lock was declared final and sent to patent counsel.

### The component brick solved the mould

A complex single casting is expensive to tool and hard to fill with a fibre composite. In January 2025 Zachary Huser redesigned the brick as two moulded halves that assemble into one unit, with a two-degree draft so the halves release; Brady Kirk printed them at full size. Later that year the founder, having taught himself SolidWorks, took the idea further: a brick of fourteen small parts, each moulded simply and put together quickly, a bit like a puzzle box, with six of the inserts acting as clamps that hold the halves together. A provisional patent on the approach was filed on 9 December 2025. A one-centimetre toy version followed, so that whole structures could be built in the hand.

### 2026 turned one brick into a system

Between January and June 2026 about a thousand model bricks in some fifty types were printed, and friends and advisors spent an afternoon building with them (see the party). What makes SeaBrick unique is the smooth side locking, which together with the hexagonal geometry gives connected bricks a structural fluidity. The final brick is a hexagon about one and a half times as wide as it is tall, with a lock on each of its six sides. It is laid flat in mats, stacked in rings for tubes and towers, and tied course to course with double-height bridges. In September 2026 the production design, made for a compression press, was completed. The shape is settled.

## The UBC years: six students, a press and a block that floated

  
SeaBrick's first material was made at UBC: a Capstone team in 2022, a research agreement in 2023, and the kelp composite that came to be called Kelpstone.

This is the story of the material before the brick had a shape. It comes from the project's updates of 2022 and 2023 and the photographs taken in the UBC labs.

### The idea changed from a block to a composite in one evening

The earliest SeaBrick was going to be a ceramic block. In August 2021, after a long dinner with an engineer, that was set aside for something simpler: compress kelp fibre with a natural binder, the way hardboard is made from wood, and form it in a mould. Could such a material be strong enough to build with and light enough to float? That question went to the University of British Columbia.

### Six students made a composite stronger than concrete

In the autumn of 2022 six Materials Engineering students took SeaBrick on as their Capstone project. By late November their strongest composite had a density close to water, which is why the brick became hollow. In January 2023 the team reported a kelp composite stronger than concrete in compression.

June 2023: chopped kelp feedstock loaded into a nine-cavity press mould.

### A research agreement gave the material a name

In May 2023 the project signed a sponsored research agreement with UBC's BioProducts Institute. Two summer students worked on the chemistry and the manufacturing: alginate binders, taking the alginate out of raw kelp, and the first bio-based coatings. By August the material had a name, Kelpstone, and lab specimens were testing at 57 to 60 megapascals in compression, three times a typical concrete at half the weight.

Kelp drying before processing, July 2023.

A pressed Kelpstone cube, October 2023.

That autumn three more Capstone teams, about twenty students across Manufacturing, Mechanical and Materials Engineering, took on the brick shell, the manufacturing process and a floating solar platform. More than twenty students over nine course terms have worked on SeaBrick since, and several stayed with the project after they graduated.

### What it taught us

The UBC work showed that a plant-fibre composite could be stronger than concrete. It also showed what was left: keeping water out, and finding fibre at scale. Those two problems shaped everything after, including the move from seaweed to flax told in a later article.

# Page: journal/two-coasts

## A research year on two coasts

  
In 2024 the materials work moved to Perth, the brick's shape took a large step with the hinge-lock, and a small Ocean Supercluster grant brought the research back to Vancouver for 2025.

This comes from the December 2024 update sent to the project's supporters. By the end of 2023 we had a material, a lease on a small manufacturing space on Venables Street in Vancouver, and more questions than people. 2024 was the year the research spread out.

### Perth showed that the fibre could be any seaweed

Much of the materials work moved to Perth, where director Ory Lavallee and engineer Chris Fisher carried on from the UBC team with support from the Western Australian government. They found a simpler and faster process with a slightly different formulation. They also showed that a composite made with sargassum, the seaweed that piles up on Caribbean and West African beaches, reached the same strength as kelp. For a material whose supply chain was its weakest link, that mattered.

### The hinge-lock made the joint a turn instead of a push

At the same time the shape of the brick changed. Working with Brady Kirk and Xiaoyuan Geng on 3D design and printing, the team replaced the earlier tongue-and-bump lock with the hinge-lock. Bricks could be joined and separated with far less force and held more securely. In September 2024 the hinge-lock was declared final for patent purposes. Children took to it at once, which turned out to be a useful test.

### A small grant brought the work home

Late in 2024 Canada's Ocean Supercluster awarded the project a $25,000 Ocean Startup grant to validate a new waterproofing strategy: one that does not require petrochemicals as an input and does not risk releasing microparticles into the sea. A team of UBC Materials Engineering students would run it in 2025 under two of the project's engineering advisors. That study is the next article.

### Other threads of 2024

The year also brought three patent filings on kelp processing and farming, a pilot of advance "claims" on future bricks, grants from the Arbitrum Foundation and Elephant Room for the digital platform behind those claims, and a letter of intent with a coastal First Nation about a prototype floating building. The threads that continued are on the story page.

# Page: journal/when-the-seaweed-did-not-arrive

2025 · Materials
  

## When the seaweed did not arrive

  
The year SeaBrick learned that a brilliant material is only as good as its supply chain, and found that flax and hemp could do the job the kelp had been doing.

By 2025 the project had a kelp composite that worked on the bench. What it did not have was kelp. This article comes from the 2025 year-end review.

### There was no seaweed fibre industry to buy from

The de-alginated fibre from Haida Gwaii was expensive and varied from batch to batch. A container of processed seaweed ordered from Southeast Asia took more than six months to arrive and was poor when it did. At the International Seaweed Symposium in Victoria that year the picture became clear: nobody in North America made seaweed fibre at scale, and nobody was about to. We were trying to build a material industry on top of a farming industry that did not exist yet.

### Flax and hemp did the same job, from fields that already exist

In June, twenty kilograms of flax fibre arrived from a Prairie processor, and hemp followed in July. The results were immediate. Flax and hemp outperformed the seaweed composite in every strength test, to the point of maxing out the compression press, from a fibre that Canada grows and processes every year by the tonne.

Carbon, the original reason for choosing kelp, was not lost. All organic fibre is cellulose, and flax is 40 to 45% carbon. Each SeaBrick still sequesters more than its own weight in CO2.

### Kelp stayed in the vision

The kelp farming and processing patents remain, and so does the long-term picture of coastal communities growing their own building material. By the end of 2025 the brick was made mainly of hemp and flax, and by late 2026 the material had moved again, to a mineral foam with the fibre mineralizing inside it. That story is here.

# Page: journal/why-a-nonprofit

## Bricks are the means, not the end

  
SeaBrick Society was formed in February 2022. Here is what it is for, and why a brick needs a nonprofit.

This is the first article in the journal, and it answers the question people ask first: why is a brick project a nonprofit? The short answer is that the brick was never the point.

### The bigger objective is self-sustaining ocean communities

One third of the world's people live on the coast. Rising seas will take the homes of hundreds of millions of them in the next thirty years, and most of them cannot afford a ticket to Mars. SeaBrick is a way for coastal communities to build out into the water instead of retreating from it. Docks and platforms first; then floating homes, and in time whole communities. Bricks are just the hardware from which those communities will be built.

### A mission like that needs an owner that cannot be bought

A company has to sell, and sooner or later a company gets sold. So on 17 February 2022 SeaBrick Society was incorporated as a Canadian not-for-profit with one purpose;
To promote innovation and standards around environmentally sustainable marine infrastructure building materials.

The Society holds the SeaBrick form and the research. Related companies make and sell what comes out of it. The mission stays where it cannot be bought.

### Community ownership, community control

The long-term vision is a decentralized, cooperative manufacturing ecosystem: low-tech production plants that ship in a container, so that communities affected by climate change can build their own floating infrastructure from materials at hand. The means of long-term production belong with the people who need it most. That is what the Society is for, and the rest of the journal is the record of getting there.
